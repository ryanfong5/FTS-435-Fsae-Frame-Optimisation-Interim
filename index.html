FTS-435: Optimisation of the R27e FSAE Frame

R26e was built to be light but failed the rain test and never ran the dynamic events. We redesigned the R27e frame to keep water out, open up access and pass inspection first, while still cutting mass.

1.2 What went wrong with R26e Shared

A car must pass five inspections before it may run dynamic events: mechanical/accumulator, EV active, tilt, rain and brake.

In the rain test, water is sprayed at the car from every direction for 120 s with the high-voltage system on, and the car is watched for another 120 s. If the insulation monitoring device detects a leak from high voltage to the chassis at any point, the car fails [1].

R26e failed the rain test, so it ran no dynamic event. We traced two frame-related causes:

  1. Poor waterproofing. The rear electronics sat under a duct-taped shroud, with a gap around the maintenance switch (MSD). Water reached the electronics through these gaps.
  2. Poor access. The main roll hoop, mounted inside the monocoque, blocked the electronics, and five harnesses crossed the car. Fixing one part meant removing others, so sealing and checking the bay was slow.

1.4 Why mass still matters Shared

Compliance and integration get the car onto the track, but they do not win points on it. FSAE is still a race: dynamic points go to the fastest cars, and the field gets faster every year. A car designed only to meet the rules would fall behind, so mass stays a design goal, cut wherever the first two gates allow.

Lower mass gives faster acceleration (F = ma) and better cornering through lower yaw inertia and quicker weight transfer. The team's lap-time simulation shows the trend:

2.1 Front monocoque shrink Wong Yi Tung Shanice

The R26e front monocoque was larger than the rules require. We shrank it from the bottom and sides toward the FSAE internal cross-section template: a 350 mm × 350 mm shape that must pass through the cockpit to prove there is room for the driver's legs [1]. A smaller cross-section means less laminate area, and so less mass.

Rollover protection kept

The rules need straight lines from the top of the main hoop to the top of the front hoop to stay clear of the driver's helmet. Shrinking from the bottom and sides does not lower the front hoop, so these lines and the driver's seating position are unchanged.

Space for the front wing

A narrower, lower nose leaves more room under and beside it for the front wing, which the aerodynamics team can use for downforce.

Packaging around the template

We placed the template in CAD first, then checked that the steering column, pedal box and dashboard fit around it without clashing.

Mass: laminate falls from 10.23 kg to 9.24 kg (−989.58 g).

Pros
  • Lighter monocoque (−0.99 kg)
  • More room for aerodynamic parts
  • Still inside the rule template and rollover lines
Cons
  • Heavier front suspension (+430.8 g)
  • Tighter packaging for steering, pedals and dashboard
  • Needs a new mould (Section 6)

2.2 Front roll hoop Wong Yi Tung Shanice

The front roll hoop follows the new, narrower monocoque. It is 171.01 g lighter (−8.88%): the hoop itself saves 174.21 g, and its foam core gains 3.2 g.

Pros
  • Lighter (−171 g)
  • Matches the new monocoque shape
Cons
  • New bend geometry and new welding jig needed (3.3)

2.3 Integrated rear monocoque shelf Fong Ken Khai

We replaced R26e's separate shroud and electronics shelf with one composite shelf laid up as part of the rear monocoque. It does four jobs.

1. Waterproofing

A solid surface replaces the taped shroud.

Pros
  • Far fewer water entry paths
  • Permanent seal: no tape or cover to fail
  • Leaks can only occur at known pass-throughs
  • Cables pass through waterproof Autosport connectors
Cons
  • Adds 1.13 kg of laminate
  • More complex mould and layup
  • Underside hidden once electronics are fitted

2. Electronics mounting

The Cascadia motor controller bolts straight on, removing the 1,090 g mounting shelf.

Pros
  • Controller and electronics easy to reach
  • Shorter cable runs
  • Stiffer controller mounting
  • Enables electrical packaging savings (5.5)
Cons
  • Damage cannot be fixed by swapping a part
  • Controller heat sits on the laminate

3. Shorter roll hoop braces

The braces land on the shelf instead of the rear corners of the monocoque, so they are shorter.

Pros
  • Lighter braces (1,299 → 865 g)
  • Flat landing, easier to jig
Cons
  • Shelf needs extra layup where braces land

4. Shoulder harness mounting

The harness bolts directly to the shelf, removing the 831.61 g steel harness bar.

Pros
  • Less welding
  • Bolts easy to inspect
Cons
  • Needed a 30 kN proof test (failed twice first)
  • Local reinforcement adds mass
  • Damaged potted inserts are hard to repair
  • More pull tests needed to confirm the result

2.4 Main roll hoop redesign Fong Ken Khai

Moved outside the monocoque

On R26e the main roll hoop passed through the monocoque skin. That left gaps around the tubes and blocked the electronics. On R27e the hoop sits outside and bolts on, so the skin stays closed.

New shape for helmet clearance

The rules need at least 50 mm between the helmet and two straight lines: from the top of the main hoop to the top of the front hoop, and to the lower end of the main hoop bracing [1]. Widening the whole single-bend hoop would have added a lot of tube. Instead, the R27e hoop changes shape near the top to clear the helmet. The hoop gains only 67.98 g (+2.2%), going from 3,118.96 g to 3,186.94 g.

3. Prototypes & fabrication A3

3.1 Shoulder harness pull-test jig Fong Ken Khai

Purpose: to prove that the new panel-mounted shoulder harness attachment holds 30 kN, as the SES requires. The team's old jig was built for about 15 kN.

  • Constraints: limited space and grip layout in the universal testing machine (UTM), and a fixed composite test-panel size.
  • Design: a new fixture clamps the panel on all sides, so it behaves like the monocoque wall around the mount rather than a loose plate. It has a stronger clevis and bolts for 30 kN.
  • Check: FEA of the jig to find the highest-stress areas before machining.

3.2 Composite test panels Wong Yi Tung Shanice

Panels and inserts

Sandwich panels were laid up to the R27e shelf layup, so the test matches the car. Potting compound fills the honeycomb cells around each insert, so the bolt load spreads into both skins instead of crushing the core.

Backing plate

A backing plate spreads the bolt load over more skin. Its size was limited on two sides:

  • Area: other mounting holes sit close by on the shelf, so the plate could not simply be made larger.
  • Thickness: space behind the panel is tight, so the plate could not simply be made thicker.

We therefore tried different materials and sizes to find the best fit within both limits. Roark's formula for a circular plate under a central load P gives the thinnest plate that will not yield [8]. Here σy is the plate yield stress, ν is Poisson's ratio, and a and b are the outer and loaded radii:

t = √( 3P / (2π σy) · [ (1 + ν) ln(a / b) + 1 ] )

Bending at VR3

The tubes are bent by VR3 Engineering, which limits the geometry we can draw:

  • Bend radius: each bend is made around a fixed die, so the centreline radius must match a die VR3 has. Standard 1″ tube dies are 3.25″ or 5.88″ (Appendix J).
  • Straight lengths: the bender must grip the tube, so 1″ tube needs at least 4.50″ of straight tube between bends and 3.00″ at each end.
  • Thin walls: thinner tubes need larger radii to avoid flattening or buckling.

We modelled each hoop following VR3's CAD guidelines: every bend tangent to the straight sections either side, and no notch or trim inside a bend [10].

Welding jig

A modular welding jig holds both hoops and their braces in their true positions relative to the monocoque while they are welded.

4. Testing & results A4A5

4.1 Shoulder harness pull test Fong Ken Khai

Why: the rules require harness mounts on a monocoque to be proven by a physical test, not by calculation alone [1][2]:

F.7.9.1 Required loads“Each attachment point for the Shoulder Belts must support a minimum load of 15 kN before failure”, with a required load of 30 kN shared between the two shoulder belt attachments.
F.7.9.2 Load testing
  • The required load is applied to a representative attachment, using the proposed layup and bracket.
  • The test fixture's edges must be at least 125 mm from the load point.
  • The shoulder belt load must be applied at the angle set in the rules, not simply straight up.
  • The sample cannot be larger than the as-built monocoque section, and its width cannot exceed the shoulder harness panel height listed in the SES.
The intent is that the test panel matches the competition car as closely as possible.

Setup: composite panel clamped in the custom jig on the UTM and pulled until it failed or the test was stopped, logging force and stroke every 0.01 s. Three tests, one specimen each:

The chart plots force against how far the machine has moved. A steady rise means the panel is carrying load; a sudden drop means something has slipped, cracked or crushed.

What the curves show

  • Test 1: force levelled off at 12–13 kN and crept to 14.15 kN, suggesting the panel slipped in the loose clamp.
  • Test 2: force rose steadily to 17.89 kN at 18.3 mm, then fell in steps as the core crushed.
  • Test 3: force reached 24.6 kN, dropped to 12.6 kN, then recovered and climbed to 30.10 kN, where the test was stopped.

What this means

  • The reinforced design meets the 30 kN requirement. The 2T4UD panel, not the jig, was the weak point.
  • In Tests 2 and 3 the insert held. Both panels bent up into the jig opening and the core crushed near the clamped edges. Thicker local skins and extra potting raised that failure from 17.89 kN to above 30 kN.

Why the reinforcement worked

  • Thicker skins make a stiffer panel. A sandwich panel's bending stiffness rises with the thickness of its skins, because the skins carry the bending load and the core only holds them apart. Going from 2T4UD to 4T8UD roughly doubles the skin, so the panel bends less at the same load and passes less of it into the core. Failure-mode studies of honeycomb sandwich beams show that thicker skins move failure to a higher load [11].
  • More potting spreads the load. Potting turns the honeycomb around the insert into a solid plug, so the pull is shared over a wider area of both skins. Research on potted inserts shows that a larger potted area raises both stiffness and failure load, and moves damage away from the core and skins [12][13].
  • The clamp edge is the weak spot. The tight clamp acts as a rigid edge. Bending and core shear are highest exactly where the free panel meets the clamp, which is where the core crushed in both tests. Stiffer skins lower the peak there, so the core lasted longer.

4.2 Honeycomb core comparison Shared

Why: rising shipping costs and a large minimum order quantity meant we could no longer get the usual Corex aluminium honeycomb. We sourced an alternative from Huarui Honeycomb Technology (Foshan, China): 3003-H18 aluminium, 6.4 mm cells, 0.08 mm foil, 84 kg/m³, perforated (spec sheet in Appendix G). We tested it against Corex before using it.

  • Specimens: 25 mm honeycomb core with carbon skins on both faces, same layups for each core (2T1UD, 2T2UD, 2T4UD).
  • Punch shear: a punch is pushed through the panel, like a bolt pulling through a mount.
  • Three-point bending: panel on two supports, loaded in the middle, for bending strength and stiffness.

Results

StrengthCostLead timeAvailability
  • Punch shear: the cores are within 4% at every layup (−0.8% to +3.7%). Layup matters far more: 2T4UD nearly triples the 2T1UD load.
  • Bending: the alternative core peaked 5–17% higher, with almost the same initial slope (about 1.2 and 1.5 kN/mm), so similar stiffness.
  • Conclusion: the Huarui core is selected for the R27e monocoque. Compared with Corex it offers:
    • Equal or better strength: within 4% in punch shear and 5–17% higher in bending, across all layups tested.
    • Lower cost: cheaper per sheet, with cheaper shipping to Singapore.
    • Shorter lead time: 15 days from order, with regional shipping to Singapore.
    • Better availability: a wide range of cell sizes, foil gauges and thicknesses made to order.

6. Monocoque mould A2

The narrower front and the integrated shelf mean the R26e mould no longer fits. We first explored modifying it, then compared it with two new moulds.

6.1 Modifying the R26e mould Shared

The first option considered was to adapt the existing R26e mould rather than build a new one. Two changes would have been needed.

Narrowing the front with PU inserts

Polyurethane (PU) foam inserts would be machined and fixed inside the old mould to form the narrower R27e front. The inserts follow compound curves, which are difficult to machine accurately, and their thin edges are prone to damage during layup.

Adding the shelf by secondary bonding

The R26e mould has no shelf, so the shelf would be moulded as a separate part and bonded to the cured monocoque. Because the bonded joint becomes part of the primary structure, the SES judges require tensile tests to prove that it is at least as strong as the parent laminate, on both the inner and outer skins (Appendix F).

Outcome

Together, the inserts and the bonding qualification made this route complex and costly: extra machining, extra parts, a separate test programme, and the risk of a weak joint in the main structure. A shelf moulded in one piece with the monocoque has no bonded joint and avoids all of this. We therefore looked at building a new mould, compared in 6.2.

6.2 Building a new mould Shared

Two ways of building a new mould were compared. Both produce the full R27e shape, including the shelf, in one piece.

Option A: Carbon fibre mould from a machined plug

A full-size plug of the monocoque is machined from PU tooling board. A carbon fibre mould is then laid up over the plug, cured and removed. This is the conventional route for composite tooling.

Pros
  • High stiffness, so the mould holds its shape under vacuum
  • Expands at a similar rate to the carbon part during cure
  • Smooth surface finish and reliable vacuum seal
  • Long tool life over many layups
Cons
  • Two stages (plug, then mould), so a long lead time [5]
  • A large block of tooling board is machined away
  • High material and labour cost
  • Any later change to the shape needs a new plug and mould

Option B: 3D-printed mould Selected

The mould is printed directly in RTP 385, a polycarbonate filled with 30% carbon fibre [3]. It is too large to print in one go, so it is split into four sections, printed, bonded together and then machined to the final surface. The carbon fibre filler makes the printed polymer stiffer and more stable than plain plastic.

Pros
  • Printed straight from CAD, with no full plug to machine
  • Only the material needed is printed, so less waste [5]
  • Shorter lead time for the tool [4][6]
  • Easy to reprint a section if the shape changes in later seasons
Cons
  • Polymer can deform in a long, hot cure; the cure must stay well below its 138 °C deflection temperature (Appendix I)
  • Printed surfaces need machining and sealing for finish and vacuum [4]
  • Sections must be bonded accurately
  • Expensive print material and long print time

Decision

The carbon fibre mould scored highest on stiffness and accuracy, but its two-stage process makes it the slowest and most expensive. The 3D-printed mould gives the same one-piece shape without a plug, is quicker to produce and can be updated for future cars. We selected it, and manage its risks by bonding the sections carefully, machining the final surface, and keeping the cure temperature within the material's limit.

7.2 Key challenges

Four problems shaped the project this semester.

  • Redesigning the monocoque. R26e's shortcomings meant redesigning the monocoque around the shelf, relocated main hoop and narrower front, while still fitting every component (Section 2).
  • Harness panel below 30 kN. The first R27e panel failed at 17.89 kN. Local 4T8UD reinforcement and extra potting raised it to 30.10 kN (4.1).
  • Core supply. Corex became too costly; testing showed the Huarui core matches it (4.2).
  • Tooling. Adapting the R26e mould was too complex and costly, so a 3D-printed mould was chosen (Section 6).

    When the monocoque returns, notch the roll hoop tubes, set up the welding jig, lathe inserts for all holes, and machine the mounts and backing plates.

  3. Finish the roll hoops
    Feb 2027

    Fully weld the roll hoops and send them for powder coating.

  4. Prove waterproofing
    Feb 2027

    Build a rain test rig and pass the rain test on the monocoque before competition.

7.4 Timeline

SES acceptance comes first, because the final layup cannot be fixed until the judges accept it. The monocoque is the longest task: the roll hoops cannot be welded in place, or the wiring started, until it arrives.

Milestones: SES accepted (end Nov) → monocoque received (end Dec) → roll hoops welded (early Feb) → rain test passed (mid Feb) → R27e shakedown (end Feb 2027).

F. Secondary bonding test requirements (SES judges, FSAE 2027)
  • Use the production layups and the production secondary-bonding procedure.
  • Test skin-only coupons, without honeycomb core.
  • Place the bond across the coupon's gauge section.
  • Compare ultimate tensile load and strength between bonded and unbonded coupons.
  • Record failure load, failure location and failure mode.
  • Show that the bonded joint matches or exceeds the parent laminate.

Not required: three-point bending, and perimeter or punch shear of the joint. Lap-shear and T-peel tests were not addressed in the judges' reply.

Minimum: four specimen configurations (two parent controls, two bonded). Repeats are recommended; the judges set no minimum count.
